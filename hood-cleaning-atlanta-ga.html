The standard

NFPA 96 compliant, and what that means here

The work is an NFPA 96 compliant cleaning of the exhaust path. NFPA 96 is the standard for ventilation control and fire protection of commercial cooking operations. It is the reason the hood, the filters, the duct, and the fan are cleaned on a schedule, and the reason the kitchen keeps a record. The cleaning is NFPA 96 compliant when the path that can be reached is actually cleaned and the record says what was done. This page does not call the company or the kitchen certified.

What an NFPA 96 compliant visit covers

Hood cleaning Atlanta GA and kitchen exhaust cleaning Atlanta GA are the same exhaust path on this visit. The scope is the metal grease travels through, not a wipe of the face of the canopy.

  • Hoods. The canopy shell, the plenum behind the filters, the grease troughs, and the grease cups are scraped and washed. Grease on the outside lip is part of the hood.
  • Filters. Baffle filters come out and get washed. Mesh filters, where a hood still has them, are called out because they hold grease instead of shedding it.
  • Ductwork. Horizontal runs and vertical risers are cleaned from the hood, from the access panels, and from the fan end when that end is open. A length with no opening is written as not entered.
  • Rooftop exhaust fans. When the roof is safe to work, the fan is hinged and the wheel and housing are cleaned. A hinge that is seized is reported, not forced until something breaks.
  • Access panels. The crew opens the panels that exist and leaves them so they can be opened again. A missing or painted-shut panel is a limit on the report, not a section that was cleaned.
  • Before and after photo documentation. Photos of the hood, the opened duct access, and the fan stay with the post-service report. Three frames lower on this page are still waiting on real photos.
  • Certificate stickers. The sticker on the hood names TruShine Services and the service date. It is the cleaning record. It is not a suppression tag, and it is not a certification of the kitchen.

Inspection frequency by cooking volume

The interval follows cooking volume, not the ZIP code. The reading published on the NFPA 96 compliance page for Atlanta follows Georgia's fire prevention code Table 606.3.3.1 this way:

  • Solid fuel: every month.
  • High-volume cooking, including 24-hour cooking, charbroiling, or wok cooking: every 3 months.
  • Other cooking operations, including a typical restaurant line: every 6 months.
  • Low-volume cooking, such as places of worship, seasonal businesses, and senior centers: every 12 months.

Grease found on the hood, the filters, the duct, or the fan is cleaned even if a date on an old certificate sticker has not arrived. As published, that table is an inspection frequency. Cleaning follows when grease has accumulated. Most Atlanta restaurants that cook every day clean on that same rhythm so the inspection does not find a loaded duct.

Inside the city limits, the fire authority to ask is the Atlanta Fire Rescue Department. A health inspector is a separate visit and is looking at the kitchen around the hood, not writing the exhaust file. Decatur, Brookhaven, Sandy Springs, Dunwoody, Marietta, Roswell, Alpharetta, and the other cities below have their own fire authorities. The fire official at that address can shorten the interval. This page is a plain description of the standard, not a legal opinion, and it does not guess which edition a given station is carrying. Confirm the edition before you rely on a row.

Grease in the trap and oil in the fryer are different containers. Those jobs are on the grease trap page and the used cooking oil page. Nozzles, links, and cylinders over the line are suppression work, booked on their own. A longer note on the standard sits on the NFPA 96 compliance page for Atlanta.

The visit

The full pass, from inspection to the sticker

Every Atlanta hood cleaning follows the same sequence, whether the fan is on a Midtown setback or a one-story curb in East Atlanta. The building changes the access. It does not change the list.

Inspection

Before degreaser goes on anything, the crew looks at the hood, the filter bank, the plenum, the access panels, and the path to the rooftop exhaust fan. The note records how the kitchen cooks, whether the roof can be worked, and which panels actually open. A price is not a guess made from the street.

Hood

The canopy shell is scraped and washed, including the grease troughs and grease cups. Grease on the outside lip is part of the hood, not a separate favor. If the plenum behind the filters is loaded, that cavity is in the same pass. A wipe of the face of the hood is not written up as a finished hood.

Filters

Baffle filters come out and get washed. Mesh filters, where a hood still has them, are called out because they hold grease instead of shedding it. A filter wash by itself is not a full exhaust cleaning, and the report does not pretend that it is.

Degreasing

Degreasing is the chemical and hand work on the metal the crew can touch: hood, plenum, panel edges, fan housing, and the duct interior that was opened. The cookline is covered first. Cups and containers that belong to the hood are emptied. Surfaces that are not part of the exhaust path stay covered.

Access panels

Duct cleaning stops at openings that are really there. Older in-town risers sometimes have no safe panel, or a panel that has been sealed over. The crew opens the panels that exist and leaves them so they can be opened again. A missing panel is written as a limit, not as a section that was cleaned.

Ductwork

Horizontal runs over a Buckhead line and vertical risers in Downtown or Midtown are different jobs. The crew cleans the duct that can be reached from the hood, from the access panels, and from the fan end when that end is open. A length with no opening stays on the report as not entered.

Rooftop exhaust fan

When the roof is safe to work, the fan is hinged and the wheel and housing are cleaned. A hinge that is seized is reported, not forced until something breaks. A bright canopy with a still-loaded fan is an unfinished exhaust cleaning. Screen walls and setbacks can make the fan the long part of the night.

Post-service report and certificate sticker

The kitchen gets a certificate sticker and a written post-service report naming TruShine Services, the service date, and the parts actually opened. Before and after photos stay with that file. The certificate sticker is the hood record. It is not a suppression tag, it is not a certification of the kitchen, and it does not forecast the next inspection.
