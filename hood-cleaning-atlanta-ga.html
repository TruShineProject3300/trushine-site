Why hood cleaning is a city problem, not a generic one

Drive from Sweet Auburn to West Midtown and the hoods change with the buildings. Downtown and Midtown stacks rise through several floors before they reach a fan. East Atlanta and the Old Fourth Ward are full of one-story shells where the duct is short and the roof curb is close to a neighbor's intake. Buckhead dining rooms tend to run longer canopies over several appliances. The cleaning has to follow the metal, not a single script.

Summer humidity keeps rooftop grease soft. A fan that looked acceptable in March can load up by July if the line is frying every night. Late kitchens along Edgewood Avenue and Memorial Drive add hours of cooking after a lunch-only cafe in the same ZIP code has already shut down. Those two businesses should not be on the same calendar just because they share a city name.

Fire code

What Atlanta Fire Rescue is working from

Georgia adopts its minimum fire code through the Safety Fire Commissioner's rules, including Chapter 120-3-3. The state's fire prevention code, in the edition based on the 2024 International Fire Code, covers commercial cooking systems in Section 606. Table 606.3.3.1 is an inspection schedule, keyed to how the kitchen cooks:

  • Solid-fuel appliances: inspect every month.
  • High-volume cooking, including 24-hour operations, charbroiling, and wok cooking: inspect every 3 months.
  • Other commercial cooking: inspect every 6 months.
  • Low-volume cooking, including places of worship, seasonal businesses, and senior centers: inspect every 12 months.

The code then says that if grease has built up on the hood, filters, fan, duct, or other parts, those parts get cleaned. The fire code official can shorten the interval. Inside Atlanta, that official sits with Atlanta Fire Rescue. This page is a plain-language reading of the published state table, not a legal opinion, and it is not a promise that every station is on the newest edition. Ask the inspector which edition they are enforcing before you debate a date on a sticker.

A fuller walk-through of the standard and the paperwork sits on the NFPA 96 compliance page for Atlanta. The exhaust page covers duct and fan access, which is a different question from how often the hood is due.

The visit

What an Atlanta hood cleaning includes

Canopy and filters

Baffle filters come out and get washed. The canopy and plenum are degreased. Mesh filters, where a hood still has them, get called out because they are a poor grease stop.

Accessible duct

We clean the duct we can reach through the panels that exist. Older in-town buildings sometimes have a riser with no safe opening. That limit goes in the notes.

Rooftop fan

When the roof is safe to work, the fan is hinged, the wheel and the housing are cleaned, and the hinge is left working. A shiny canopy with a loaded fan is not a finished hood.

Protection and photos

Nearby surfaces are covered before degreaser is used. Before and after photos show the canopy, the filters, and the fan.

A dated record

You get a written note with the company name and the service date. That is the record Section 606 expects to exist, kept on site with the suppression tag.

A straight scope

We do not describe a filter wash as a full system cleaning. If the fan was locked out or the roof was unsafe, the report says the fan was not cleaned.

Suppression nozzles, links, and cylinders are not part of this hood cleaning. Those belong to a suppression inspection. See fire suppression if that tag is the item that is about to lapse.

A practical benefit

What you actually get out of the visit

The useful outcome is simple. Grease is off the metal we could reach, and a person in the restaurant can show a date when the inspector asks. There is no scoreboard attached to that. A clean hood does not by itself pass a health inspection, and it does not renew a suppression certificate.

Managers who run two or three Atlanta stores often want the same paperwork in each kitchen. We can put those stores on one schedule if the cooking volume matches. If one store charbroils and another only reheats, they should not share a calendar. Say so when you book.

Night and weekend starts are available when the dining room needs the line the next morning. The crew still needs roof access, a working fan hinge, and someone who can shut the appliances down.

Questions

Atlanta hood cleaning questions

Who inspects kitchen hoods inside the city of Atlanta?

Inside the city limits, the Atlanta Fire Rescue Department is the fire authority. A health inspector is a separate visit and looks at the kitchen around the hood, not the exhaust path the fire code describes.

How often does Georgia expect an Atlanta hood to be checked?

The Georgia State Minimum Fire Prevention Code table for commercial cooking systems uses the cooking type: monthly for solid fuel, every 3 months for high-volume cooking such as 24-hour, charbroiling, or wok lines, every 6 months for other kitchens, and every 12 months for low-volume uses such as places of worship, seasonal businesses, and senior centers. If grease is present, the system is cleaned. Your inspector can require a tighter clock.

Is a hood cleaning the same as a suppression inspection?

No. The hood, filters, duct, and fan are the exhaust cleaning. The wet-chemical system over the appliances has its own service interval and its own tag. A clean hood does not renew that tag.

Can you clean hoods in older in-town buildings?

Yes, with the access the building actually has. Virginia-Highland, Old Fourth Ward, and Grant Park storefronts often have short roof runs and tight panels. If a section of duct cannot be reached safely, the service note says so instead of implying the whole run was opened.

What should be on file when the fire inspector asks?

A dated cleaning record that names the company and the date, plus the separate suppression tag. Photos from the visit help a manager show what the fan and the canopy looked like that day.

Do you only work inside the city limits?

The Atlanta page is for kitchens in the city and close-in neighborhoods such as East Point, College Park, and Decatur. Marietta, Roswell, and Alpharetta have their own fire authorities, and those cities have their own pages.
