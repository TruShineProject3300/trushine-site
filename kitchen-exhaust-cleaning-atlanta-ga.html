The full system

Past the filters, all the way to the roof

A clean canopy can hide a loaded duct. Grease vapor that gets past the filters cools and settles on the inside of the plenum, the horizontal duct, the vertical riser, and the fan wheel. That layer is out of sight, and it is fuel. If a fire on the cookline gets into the duct, it can travel up the system to the roof. Kitchen exhaust cleaning is the work that keeps that path clean.

This page is about the parts of the system beyond the hood. For the hood visit as a whole, including the canopy, the filters, and the certificate sticker, see the hood cleaning Atlanta page. Both are the same NFPA 96 compliant visit from the Norcross shop.

Plenum

The cavity behind the filters is the first place grease lands after the filter bank. It is scraped and degreased before the crew moves into the duct.

Horizontal ductwork

Runs above the ceiling collect grease along the bottom of the duct. The crew cleans them from the hood, from the access panels, and from the fan end when that end is open.

Vertical risers

In Downtown and Midtown buildings the riser can climb several floors. Those sections are cleaned from the openings that exist, and anything that cannot be reached is written on the report as not entered.

Access panels

NFPA 96 calls for access openings on the duct so it can be cleaned. The crew opens the panels that exist, cleans through them, and closes them so they can be opened again. A missing or sealed panel is noted so it can be added.

Rooftop exhaust fans

When the roof is safe to work, the fan is tipped open, and the wheel, the blades, and the housing are degreased. A fan that looks fine from the outside can carry a heavy load on the wheel.

Fan hinge kits

NFPA 96 calls for upblast fans to be hinged with a flexible wiring connection so the fan can be opened for cleaning. If the fan has no hinge, or the hinge is seized, the report says so rather than forcing it.

Grease containment

Grease that leaves the fan runs onto the roof unless it is caught. The crew checks the containment unit or absorbent catch around the fan and notes when it is full, damaged, or missing.

Before and after photos

Photos of the opened duct, the access panels, and the fan wheel go with the post-service report, so a manager can show what was cleaned past the hood.

Report and certificate sticker

The post-service report lists the duct sections, panels, and fan, and marks anything not reached. The certificate sticker names TruShine Services and the service date. It is not a suppression tag.

NFPA 96

How often the exhaust system is checked

NFPA 96 sets the inspection schedule for grease buildup by cooking volume, in Table 12.4 in recent editions. When the inspection finds grease, the system is cleaned.

  • Solid fuel cooking: monthly.
  • High-volume cooking, such as 24-hour cooking, charbroiling, or wok cooking: quarterly.
  • Moderate-volume cooking: semiannually.
  • Low-volume cooking, such as churches, day camps, seasonal businesses, or senior centers: annually.

The schedule covers the whole system: the hood, the filters, the ductwork, and the exhaust fan. Inside the city limits, the fire authority is the Atlanta Fire Rescue Department, and the fire marshal at a given address can require more than the minimum. Decatur, Sandy Springs, Marietta, Norcross, and the other metro cities have their own fire authorities. This is a plain description of the standard, not legal advice. Confirm the code edition your local fire official uses. The longer note is on the NFPA 96 compliance page for Atlanta.

Questions

Atlanta kitchen exhaust cleaning questions

What is kitchen exhaust cleaning, and how is it different from hood cleaning?

Hood cleaning starts at the canopy and the filters. Kitchen exhaust cleaning follows the grease past the filters, through the plenum, the horizontal duct, the vertical riser, the access panels, and out to the rooftop exhaust fan. In Atlanta both are done on the same NFPA 96 compliant visit, and this page is about the part of the system past the hood.

Why does grease in the duct matter for fire risk?

Grease vapor that gets past the filters condenses on the inside of the duct and the fan. That layer can carry a fire from the cookline into the duct and up to the roof, where it is hard to reach. Cleaning the duct and fan on the interval set by cooking volume is how NFPA 96 keeps that fuel load down.

How often does the exhaust system need to be cleaned?

NFPA 96 sets the inspection schedule by cooking volume: monthly for solid fuel, quarterly for high-volume cooking such as 24-hour cooking, charbroiling, or wok cooking, semiannually for moderate volume, and annually for low volume such as churches and seasonal businesses. When grease is found, the system is cleaned. Atlanta Fire Rescue, or the fire authority in your city, can require more.

What if my duct has no access panels?

The crew cleans the duct that can be reached from the hood, from existing access panels, and from the fan end. A run with no opening is written on the post-service report as not entered. NFPA 96 calls for access openings on the duct, so the report notes where one is missing so you can have it added.

What is a fan hinge kit, and do I need one?

A hinge kit lets an upblast rooftop fan tip open for cleaning without lifting it off the curb or straining the wiring. NFPA 96 calls for an upblast fan to be hinged with a flexible wiring connection. If your fan has no hinge, or the hinge is seized, the report says so.

What is rooftop grease containment?

Grease that leaves the fan collects around it and runs onto the roof. A grease containment unit or absorbent catch around the fan holds it. The crew checks the containment on each visit and notes when it is full or missing.
