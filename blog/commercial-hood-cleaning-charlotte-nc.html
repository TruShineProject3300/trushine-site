Why the whole system matters

Cooking sends grease vapor up through the hood. It settles on the filters, the plenum behind them, the ductwork and the rooftop fan. NFPA 96 covers that entire path, not only the hood you can see from the line. Grease you cannot see is still fuel.

What a full exhaust clean includes

  1. Protect the kitchen. The crew wraps and tarps the cook line, the walls and the equipment before any water or degreaser comes out.
  2. Hood interior and canopy. Built up grease is scraped, degreased and washed down to clean metal.
  3. Filters. Removed, cleaned and put back, or replaced if they are damaged.
  4. Plenum. The space between the filters and the duct is where grease hides most often. It gets scraped and washed too.
  5. Ductwork. Access panels are opened and the duct run is cleaned as far as it can be reached. Panels are closed and sealed again when the job is done.
  6. Rooftop fan. The fan is hinged open, cleaned and checked, and the grease containment is cleaned out.
  7. Cleanup. Floors and surfaces are left the way the crew found them, so your morning team can start prep.
  8. Report and sticker. A photo report shows the work, and a certificate sticker on the hood shows the date.

How often it should happen

NFPA 96 sets the schedule by cooking volume and fuel: monthly for solid fuel, quarterly for high volume cooking such as 24 hour operations, charbroiling and wok cooking, twice a year for moderate volume and once a year for light cooking. Menus and hours change, so check that your schedule still fits. Your local fire marshal has the final word. See our guide to how often a hood needs cleaning.

Source: NFPA 96, Schedule of Inspection for Grease Buildup (Table 12.4 in recent editions). Your local authority having jurisdiction decides which edition applies.
