If you operate a commercial kitchen in the Southeast — whether it's a single restaurant, a hotel, a stadium, or a multi-location chain — you're managing compliance across multiple categories simultaneously: fire safety, grease management, health department standards, wastewater regulations, and more.

Most operators do this with 4, 5, or even 6 separate vendors. One company cleans the hoods. Another handles the grease trap. A third picks up used cooking oil. A fourth handles deep cleaning. A fifth inspects the fire suppression system.

Each one sends a separate invoice. Each one operates on a separate schedule. Each one defines their scope — and nobody owns the gap between them.

"The gap between vendors is exactly where compliance failures happen. And when an inspector walks in, they don't care whose vendor dropped the ball."

This guide explains exactly why restaurant operators across Georgia, Florida, North Carolina, South Carolina, Tennessee, Alabama, and Virginia are moving to a single kitchen compliance partner — and what to look for when making that change.

What Multiple Vendors Actually Cost You

Direct Costs

When you add up what each vendor charges separately — versus what a consolidated provider charges for the same scope — most operators find they're paying 15-25% more for fragmented service than they would for an integrated solution. This happens because:

  • Each vendor prices their service as a standalone engagement with their own overhead, insurance, and margin
  • There's no bundling discount across services

The harder costs to quantify are the operational ones. How Vendor Gaps Create Compliance Failures

The Two-Inspector Problem

Most restaurant operators know their health department inspector. What many don't know is that FOG compliance — fats, oils, and grease — is enforced by a completely separate agency: their local wastewater authority.

These are two different inspectors, from two different government agencies, who do not coordinate with each other. You can pass your health inspection and fail your FOG inspection on the same day.

Your health inspector checks food safety, sanitation, surface cleanliness, and temperature logs. Your wastewater inspector checks your grease trap pump-out records, pumping frequency, and licensed oil disposal documentation. If your grease trap vendor doesn't generate proper documentation, or if your UCO recycling company isn't licensed, you are at risk — regardless of how clean your kitchen looks.

Documentation Gaps

When five vendors each maintain their own documentation in their own format on their own timeline, you have five separate sources of compliance records. When an inspector asks for documentation on the spot, you need to be able to produce it immediately — from every vendor, for every service.

A consolidated vendor maintains all of your compliance documentation in one system. Every service generates a timestamped, photo-documented report that is immediately available. Your compliance calendar is managed proactively — you never wonder when your hood was last cleaned or when your grease trap is due for service.

Questions to Ask Any Compliance Vendor

Before signing any kitchen compliance contract, ask these questions:

  1. Are your hood cleaning technicians NFPA 96 certified? This is a non-negotiable requirement. Uncertified cleaning does not satisfy inspection requirements.
  2. What documentation do you provide after each service? You need timestamped, photo-documented CASR reports — not just a service sticker on the hood.
  3. Is your grease disposal licensed by the relevant state environmental agency? Unlicensed disposal creates liability for your operation.
  4. Can you service all of my locations across multiple states under one agreement? Multi-state operators need a partner with genuine multi-state presence and consistent standards.
  5. What is your emergency response time? A vendor who can't respond within two hours for a kitchen-down situation is not a compliance partner — they're a commodity cleaner.
  6. Do you track my compliance calendar proactively, or do I need to call you? A true compliance partner manages your schedule, not just responds when you call.

Frequently Asked Questions

How often does a commercial kitchen hood need to be cleaned?
Under NFPA 96, cleaning frequency depends on your cooking volume and fuel type. Solid fuel operations (wood, charcoal) require monthly cleaning. High-volume operations using natural gas or electric require quarterly cleaning. Lower-volume operations may qualify for semi-annual cleaning. A certified inspection will determine the correct frequency for your kitchen.
How often does a grease trap need to be pumped in Georgia, Florida, or North Carolina?
Most Southeast jurisdictions require grease trap pumping every 90 days maximum, regardless of the trap's apparent fill level. Some jurisdictions require more frequent service based on volume. All pump-out manifests must be retained for a minimum of 3 years. Active enforcement markets including Atlanta, Nashville, and Charlotte conduct routine compliance audits — not just complaint-driven inspections.
What is UCO management and why does it matter for compliance?
UCO stands for Used Cooking Oil. Proper disposal of used cooking oil is an environmental compliance requirement. It must be collected by a licensed contractor — unlicensed pickup, even free pickup, is an environmental violation that creates liability for your operation. Properly collected UCO is recycled into biodiesel, animal feed, and industrial base products. TruShine Environmental handles licensed UCO collection and provides full documentation for compliance purposes.

What is a CASR report?
A CASR (Cleaning and Service Record) is a digital compliance document generated after every TruShine service.
